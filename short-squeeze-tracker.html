What Is a Short Squeeze & How They Happen

A short squeeze is a market phenomenon that occurs when a heavily shorted stock begins to rise in price, forcing short sellers to buy shares to cover their positions. This buying creates additional upward pressure, driving the price even higher in a self-reinforcing cycle.

Basic Example: If 50% of a stock's float is sold short and the price starts rising, short sellers face mounting losses. To limit their losses, they must buy shares (cover) - this buying drives the price up further, triggering more short covering.

How Short Squeezes Happen (5-Step Process)

1
Heavy Short Interest Builds

Bearish traders sell shares they don't own, expecting the price to drop. When short interest exceeds 15-20% of float, conditions are ripe for a squeeze.

2
Catalyst Triggers Price Rise

Positive news, earnings beat, or buying pressure causes the stock to rise. Once price moves up, short sellers start feeling pain.

3
Short Sellers Begin Covering

As losses mount, short sellers buy shares to close positions. This creates additional demand, pushing price higher.

4
Feedback Loop Accelerates

More covering creates more buying pressure = higher price = more margin calls and forced covering. This compounds rapidly.

5
Peak and Reversal

The squeeze peaks when most shorts have covered. Price may then reverse dramatically as buyers exhaust themselves.

How to Keep a Short Squeeze Going

  • Buy call options - Forces market makers to buy underlying stock for delta hedging
  • Limit sell orders - Holders refuse to sell, creating artificial supply shortage
  • Coordinated buying - Social platforms driving FOMO

The Golden Rule: Sustained squeezes require constant new buying pressure. Once coverable shares are exhausted, the squeeze ends.

Primary

Days to Cover (DTC)

The estimated number of trading days required for all short sellers to close their positions, based on average daily trading volume. Higher values indicate more vulnerable short positions.

Short Interest ÷ Average Daily Volume
Squeeze Signal: DTC > 5-8 days indicates elevated squeeze potential
Primary

Short Interest (% of Float)

The percentage of a company's float (tradable shares) that have been sold short. This measures conviction among bearish investors.

(Shares Shorted ÷ Float Shares) × 100
Squeeze Signal: >15% of float; >20% is highly vulnerable
Critical

Short Interest / ADV Ratio

Ratio of current short interest to average daily volume. Shows how many days of avg volume would be needed to cover all shorts.

Short Interest ÷ Average Daily Volume
Squeeze Signal: >3-4 days indicates squeeze vulnerability
Important

Borrow Fee / Fee Rate

The annualized interest rate charged to borrow shares for shorting. Spikes when supply is tight.

Annual Rate (updated daily by brokers)
Squeeze Signal: >50-100% annually indicates extreme scarcity
Secondary

Cost to Borrow (CTB)

Another term for borrow fee - the actual cost for traders to maintain short positions over time.

Updated daily per broker inventory
Squeeze Signal: Rising CTB = tightening supply
Confirming

Utilization Rate

The percentage of available shares that have been borrowed. High utilization means few shares left to short.

(Shares Borrowed ÷ Shares Available) × 100
Squeeze Signal: >90% utilization = limited short capacity
Context

Institutional Ownership

Percentage of shares held by institutions. High institutional ownership reduces effective float available for covering.

Shares Held by Institutions ÷ Total Shares
Squeeze Signal: >80% institutional = tight float for covering
Confirming

On-Loss Ratio / Short Squeeze Index

Measures the potential for losses among short sellers. Higher values indicate more pain if price rises.

Short Interest × Days to Cover
Squeeze Signal: Combined metrics >100 suggests strong squeeze potential
